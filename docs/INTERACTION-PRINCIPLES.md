# One decision, several connected consequences

The owner endorses **Within reach**: its expressive drawing, cut-paper shapes, bold colour and stylised company/owner relationship. Future lessons should use that visual language to reveal the connected effects of a financial choice. This guidance extends the first ownership prototype; it does not change that prototype's scope or approve a completed financial model.

## The experience

The learner makes a meaningful choice. The illustrated scene responds, showing what moves, what is owed, and what changes later. The learner can compare another choice and see the consequences change together.

Three useful layers, when the concept calls for them:

| Layer | What the scene needs to explain |
| --- | --- |
| Immediate | Who pays, who receives, and what each party has available now. |
| Connected | Related taxes, costs, deductions, constraints or effects elsewhere. |
| Later | Changes to future benefits, rights, obligations or opportunities, with timing made visible. |

These are storytelling prompts, not three mandatory panels. Use one coherent composition. Reveal effects in a short causal sequence, then leave the resulting state visible. A later consequence must be visibly located in the future; animation order alone does not explain when it happens. Let the learner replay, switch choices and reset without losing the relationship between the parts. An unchanged outcome can be meaningful too.

## Creative responsibility

The assistant researches the mechanism, identifies the consequential effects, originates visual ideas, selects a treatment and builds it. The owner should not need to invent the metaphors or list every consequence. Develop several candidate mechanisms internally and choose the one with the clearest connection to the subject. Present a concrete result within the authorized scope; routine creative choices do not require another approval round.

Keep the expressive drawing style while varying the teaching mechanism. Movement, separation, overlap, accumulation, exposure and changes in access can each explain different relationships. A money bag, building or character earns its place by what its behaviour teaches. Avoid making every idea another filling container, elaborate miniature world or invented machine that needs its own explanation.

## Quantification inside the illustration

- Place amounts, dates and thresholds beside the subject they describe. The visual relationship carries the explanation; numbers make it precise.
- Clearly distinguish symbolic artwork from artwork encoding a quantity. If size, area or count represents an amount, define a consistent scale and verify it. Decorative growth must not suggest an unsupported numerical relationship.
- Distinguish cash available now, a tax liability, contribution room, eligibility and future pension entitlement. They are different kinds of things and need different visual behaviours.
- Preserve a fair comparison: state the shared starting basis, time horizon and relevant assumptions. Show company and personal effects when both matter.
- Use sourced rules and reproducible calculations for the stated year and jurisdiction. A symbolic prototype should not imply computed amounts or guaranteed outcomes. Keep detailed calculations and sources available on demand.

## How to interpret the owner's CPP example

Salary versus dividends, including effects on CPP, was offered as an example of connected consequences for a later lesson. It is not a required money-bag design or an instruction to build that calculator now.

The intended visual insight is that a payment choice can affect both today's money and a future benefit. A future CPP illustration must distinguish contributions from pension entitlement: CPP is not a personal bag that grows dollar-for-dollar with premiums. A salary scenario can affect pensionable earnings and future benefits subject to the applicable rules, earnings ceilings and contribution history. Dividends do not themselves generate CPP pensionable earnings; choosing dividends does not erase existing entitlement. Source and model the relevant rules before quantifying a lesson.

## Brief and review for each future build

1. Define the learner's decision and the misconception the scene should resolve.
2. Map each material consequence: cause, affected party, timing, kind of value/right, conditions and source. Select the effects necessary to explain the decision; keep secondary detail available without overwhelming the first view.
3. Choose a visual treatment and storyboard its sequence, final state and alternative choice. Record what each subject's behaviour means and where simplification could mislead.
4. Calculate the underlying states independently of the animation. Changing choices must recompute a coherent scene without duplicating cash, erasing existing rights or replaying effects as new transactions.
5. Check the illustration at phone size and in its settled state. Verify that action, consequence and timing are understandable, with short nearby labels as needed. Provide keyboard/touch equivalents, reduced motion and a concise textual account of the changed state.

The first ownership interaction and all three original style frames remain intact. This is the design contract for subsequent work, not a claim that these additional financial effects are already implemented.
