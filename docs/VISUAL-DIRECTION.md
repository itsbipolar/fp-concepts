## The direction: everyday analogies
Explain each abstract rule with **one familiar, physical, everyday scene** (a garden, a kitchen, a bridge, an oven, a
freezer), drawn in the Colorfigures style so it can be turned, played with and admired. The scene is the analogy; the
money is real money (gold coins of a stated value); the rule appears in plain words.

1. **Familiar first.** A 14-year-old recognises the scene in one second, with no labels. If you have to explain the
   object, it's the wrong object.
2. **One scene, one idea, one surprise.** The surprise is the misconception breaking (the RRSP and TFSA tie).
3. **Money is money.** Gold coins, bills, cheques, each with a stated value said once ("Each gold coin is $1,000").
   Never abstract units.
4. **The story is told in sentences.** One plain sentence under the scene, changing with each step (`fpStory`).
5. **Signs and tags name things in the scene** ("TAX", "YOURS", "$10,000", "TFSA: TAX THE SEEDS"), big enough to read.
6. **"What it means"** (the Labels switch, renamed) maps each part of the analogy to the real rule
   ("RRSP: NO TAX GOING IN").
7. **The numbers stay true** (the ledger, the brief's hand calculation). The analogy simplifies; it never lies.

## Language rules (apply to every figure, whatever the style)
- Write whole, plain sentences a 9th grader can follow. Say "you".
- Name things by what they are: "your yearly income", not "net income units"; "tax takes 30% of the seeds", not
  "after-tax contribution basis".
- Money in dollars, rounded where it doesn't matter ("$21,000"), exact where it does ("$95,323").
- Introduce a real term once, next to its plain meaning ("the OAS clawback: the government takes back part of your OAS").
- No invented units ("dots", "units"), no jargon on the scene, no unexplained abbreviations except account names
  clients already use (TFSA, RRSP, RRIF, OAS, CPP).
- Read every label out loud: if it isn't a meaningful phrase on its own ("YOUR INCOME: 122 DOTS"), rewrite it.
