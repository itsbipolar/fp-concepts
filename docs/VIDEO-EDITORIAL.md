# Editorial playbook for Within reach films

Adapted from the user's [fp-canada-vox-videos studio](../reference/vox-videos/README.md). Use with [video direction](VIDEO-DIRECTION.md), not in place of the established artwork. The user responded positively to our first nine-second motion study; it is the current motion reference, while a full financial film remains to be built.

## Who the film serves

The strongest shared focus is advanced **Canadian planning made visually understandable**. Start with business-owner families and incorporated professionals who have heard terms such as capital dividend account, estate freeze or integration without seeing what they do. Advisors are a second audience: their version explains a client-file question or a boundary worth checking. Sophisticated self-directed viewers can benefit, but do not dilute a specific story to address everybody.

Choose one audience per cut. A family film says “your company”; an advisor cut may say “your client's company.” Prefer a real planning question over a catalogue label. The assistant chooses the angle, researches the relationships and invents their visual explanation.

Topic territories carried over: the owner's money; tax and liquidity at death; retirement decisions; families and generations; and relevant rule changes. Insurance and cross-border questions can cross these territories. The [curated topic bank](../video/editorial/README.md) offers a small starting set rather than loading the entire upstream research catalogue into each task.

## Build a documentary argument

1. **Open on the relationship.** A concrete illustrated action or unresolved situation creates curiosity immediately. Make the subject legible before stacking text, narration and several motions at once.
2. **Ask one question early.** The viewer should know what the film promises to resolve. For a short this is usually within its first few seconds; treat timing as an editing judgment, not a universal retention formula.
3. **Introduce context when it becomes necessary.** A new fact should explain something the viewer has just noticed. Do not begin with a glossary.
4. **Chain causes and consequences.** Each beat answers “because of that, what changes?” or “what complicates that?” The upstream “but/so” rule is a useful editing test, not a requirement to repeat those words mechanically.
5. **Act out the mechanism.** Show a payer, recipient, changed right, elapsed time or constraint doing something intelligible. A noun mentioned in narration does not automatically need a new icon.
6. **Reveal what was missing.** The turn can expose another party, a later effect, an unchanged right or an important condition. Preserve the established subjects so the reveal deepens the scene.
7. **Earn the final sentence.** Leave the viewer with a precise, repeatable insight supported by the image. For a financial explainer, close with a readable, quiet disclaimer and material scope; the source repo's preference is a card headed “DISCLAIMER,” without a series logo or “sources in the description” on that card. This does not require adding a disclaimer to every nonfinancial style test.

The story might need 40 seconds or several minutes. Choose length after defining the visual argument and complexity. A longer film can yield shorter cuts, but each short needs its own question, complete causal context and meaningful ending.

## Story forms, not visual templates

| Form adapted from the other repo | Editorial purpose | Within reach treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Receipt / hidden mechanism | Explain a rule the viewer has heard but cannot picture. | Let a change of access, ownership or record reveal the rule; proof can stay in the backup. |
| The Ledger / comparison | Follow the same starting situation through two choices. | Keep the person and comparison basis consistent; show consequences in the scene. Do not default to columns, bars or a hero counter. |
| Two Deaths / two events | Explain different events, parties and tax or liquidity consequences over time. | Establish who owns what, then change the relevant relationship at each event. Make time explicit. |
| The Family File | Follow one invented household through several connected planning effects. | Return to a recognisable cast and composition. Limit each passage to what its current question needs. |
| Myth vs Statute | Resolve a familiar half-truth. | Let the mistaken expectation visibly meet the actual condition; use plain words rather than legal pages. |
| What Changed | Explain a dated change and whom it affects. | Preserve enough before/after context to show the consequence; verify enactment and effective date. |
| No Single Number | Explain why facts and judgment affect the outcome. | Reveal the relevant conditions and consequences without manufacturing a verdict or numerical certainty. |

Do not copy the source's cash tubes, scissors, piggy banks, iceberg or rocking chair as mandatory metaphors. Do not require the company/owner pair in every film. New subjects belong to the same tactile, expressive drawing language, with a new visual mechanism when the concept needs one.

## Quantities and the limits of the salary/dividend example

Amounts should answer a question about the illustrated action. When a cut, area, size or count encodes a value, calculate the true proportion and keep it consistent through later shots. When it is symbolic, avoid suggesting a measured proportion. A scale that tips or a money shape that grows makes a numerical claim if its movement implies an amount.

The upstream example's cash-only top-bracket comparison excludes payroll costs in the film. It is not a ready-made full comparison for our current owner scenario. Use equal company cost when that is the question; state exclusions where they affect the takeaway. Preserve our distinctions: RRSP room is permission rather than deposited savings, CPP is not a personal cash bag, and dividends do not erase earlier rights. Source and calculate the actual scenario before borrowing a number or conclusion.

## Narration and text

Aim for a calm, exact, warm documentary narrator with room for a slightly wry turn. One idea per sentence. Say what a rule does in plain language; leave statutory section numbers, formulas and technical quotes in the linked evidence. Introduce a technical term when knowing it helps the viewer, then show its meaning. Keep code-rendered text short and near its subject.

Write the picture and narration together in [VIDEO-SCRIPT.md](templates/VIDEO-SCRIPT.md). A short can use one spoken paragraph; longer work can use natural paragraphs to make revisions economical. Spell numbers and acronyms clearly for the voice provider while retaining exact display text. Link both versions to the same claim. Actual recorded duration determines the final timing; rough words-per-minute targets are only planning aids.

Keep pauses that help understanding. Do not import mandatory jitter, constant visible changes or an arbitrary maximum silence from the other repo. Inspect what the voice actually said, especially amounts and acronyms, and align captions to the final audio.

## Recurring people and originality

Fictional families can make later films easier to follow. Keep invented facts consistent in [the cast register](../video/editorial/cast.json), label worked examples as illustrative, and distinguish a time jump from a changed input. Our anonymous owner and older-owner assets carry no inherited MacGregor/Patel family facts. Real client stories are not a source for invented households.

Continue the current expressive faces and drawing style; the source repo's “no faces” restriction is not adopted. Reuse pixels where the action fits and generate a reference-led new pose when needed. Consistency is material, character and meaning, not repeating the same film with a different label. Record what is visually new in each film and what it teaches.
